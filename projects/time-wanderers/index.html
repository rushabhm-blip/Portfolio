PROBLEM Children often encounter history as isolated facts, images and artifacts. They may learn what an object is, but have limited opportunity to connect it with: The people who created and used it The environment they lived in Their everyday life and culture How societies developed and interacted How different civilizations connect to the world we live in today The gap is not information. The gap is context.

WHY Understanding history is more than remembering dates and objects. It can help children gradually develop: The goal is not to teach children more facts. WORLD AWARENESS How have different societies lived and evolved? CONTEXTUAL THINKING Why did people create things in the way they did?

Secondary Research What does museum-learning research tell us? CUriosity Smithsonian’s early-learning research specifically emphasizes active observation and questioning as ways to build curiosity and critical thinking. DEEPER THINKING Research with 8–9-year-olds in virtual museums found that children's involvement during and after an exhibit predicted learning. Conversation during the experience was particularly linked to inference and integrating information

Slide 8: SR

Secondary Research What does museum-learning research tell us? STRENGTHEN MEMORY A study of children aged 4–9 interacting with a dinosaur exhibit found that museum interaction increased curiosity, and higher curiosity was associated with better recall of prominently displayed facts. Exploration A review of children’s museum learning found that curiosity, excitement, discussion and exploration commonly support learning across museum settings.

Story Board The child approaches the interactive screen as the experience invites them to begin. The child uses a clenched-fist gesture to select a civilization as the map responds with a visual highlight. The child walks through the experience as the screen reveals the selected historical environment.

Slide 21: SR

Story Board The child spreads both open palms to zoom into an area as the screen follows the gesture. The child pauses to listen as the screen reveals the artifact and introduces its story. The child rolls both hands to move backwards through time as the archaeological site transforms into its original world. The child asks a question through gesture as the screen responds with a short contextual answer.

Slide 22: SR

Modalities of Interaction Modality : Hover & clench User Intent : “I want this” System Output : Select Modality : Zoom User Intent : “I want to look closer” System Output : Environment enlarges Modality : Roll back User Intent : “Take me back” System Output : Enter Past Modality : Walk User Intent : “I want to explore” System Output : Walk through Modality : Blow User Intent : “I want to uncover this” System Output : Soil disappears Modality : Back User Intent : “I want to go back” System Output : Previous screen Modality : Voice & mic gesture User Intent : “I want to know more” System Output : Answers

Slide 32: SR

Learning & Takeaways Transitioned from standard design mockups to a fully code-based prototype in VS Code, learning to systematically build out flows by layering assets, video, audio, and interactions. Realized that designing intuitive interactions requires rigorous iteration. Overcoming technical constraints and observing real users taught us to strip back complex inputs in favor of natural, everyday movements paired with clear visual signifiers. Managed a tight timeline by dividing specialised roles- asset creation, flow design, and technical assembly while learning to navigate the tension of divergence and prioritise collective group decisions.

Slide 33: SR

“ Just as a historic site can feel static until a guide shares its history, this project reinforced that every artifact holds a narrative. By building this experience, we realized that uncovering and connecting users to these hidden stories is what transforms a simple museum visit into something truly special.
